## Paper and Research Background

This review targets "The functional transformation of diplomatic immunity in the digital age," published by Zeynalova Shabnam on Zenodo in January 2027. The paper provides a legal analysis of how international law, particularly the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, is adapting to the rapid evolution of digital technologies. Traditionally, diplomatic immunity has been defined around physical spaces (embassy premises, residences) and physical objects (correspondence, documents, individual diplomats). However, the introduction of technologies such as cloud computing, encrypted communications, virtual embassies, and AI-based drafting has rendered these traditional categories ambiguous and subject to reclassification.

The author argues that this shift does not represent the collapse of the diplomatic immunity system, but rather a "functional transformation" of the institution. That is, although technological forms have changed, the core function of carrying out diplomatic missions remains intact, leading to a redefinition of the scope and application criteria of immunity. Through doctrinal analysis, this study explores how four key operative categories—premises, correspondence, archives, and diplomatic agents—should be reinterpreted in a digital context.

The research background references cases such as the data embassies of Estonia and Luxembourg, the metaverse embassy practice of Barbados, Israel's platform diplomacy, and the digital diplomacy of Scandinavian nations. These cases illustrate diplomatic activities taking place beyond physical territory, showcasing the challenges confronting existing international law.

## Core Problem and Authors' Claims

The core problem raised by the author is that the textual categories of the Vienna Convention are no longer clearly applicable in the digital environment. For example, "embassy premises" are defined as physical buildings, yet cloud servers lack clear physical boundaries and can be dispersed across multiple countries. While "diplomatic agent" refers to a natural person, the advent of AI drafting diplomatic documents or conducting negotiation simulations has blurred the subject and locus of responsibility for "diplomatic acts."

The author's main claims are as follows. First, diplomatic immunity is transforming while maintaining institutional continuity despite technological changes. Second, at the center of this transformation lies the "Principle of Functional Necessity." Under this principle, diplomats receive immunity necessary for their duties, and in the digital era, the guarantee of "digital functional performance" has become a more critical category of protection than "physical protection." Third, we are currently facing a trilemma among interpretative extension, bilateral precision, and accountability architecture. That is, there is a lack of clear consensus on whether to broadly interpret existing laws to encompass the digital environment, precisely define new rules through bilateral agreements, or how to design the technical and legal responsibility structure. This trilemma is not merely a theoretical discussion; it serves as an important analytical tool because each choice directly impacts the substantive effects of diplomatic immunity and international relations.

In conclusion, the author proposes that this functional transformation should be established as "functional resilience." To this end, the UN Group of Governmental Experts, the UN International Law Commission, and national foreign-service doctrines must collaborate to build a legal and technical foundation that ensures diplomatic missions can be stably performed in the digital age.

## Proposed Method and Key Mechanisms

This paper does not propose specific software algorithms or hardware technologies; rather, it presents a conceptual framework and interpretive principles on how the existing international law system should "operate" in a digital environment. Here, "technology" refers to a legal and political approach, with its core operational principle being the redefinition of "functional necessity."

The operational principles of traditional diplomatic immunity are as follows:
1. Physical premises inviolability: Embassy buildings cannot be forcibly entered by the receiving state's military.
2. Documents/records inviolability: Diplomatic documents are not subject to seizure anytime, anywhere.
3. Individual immunity: Diplomatic agents are immune from criminal and civil proceedings.

The author's analysis of how these principles should be reconstructed in a digital environment follows these logical steps:

1. **Virtualization of Premises**: Should cloud servers receive protection equivalent to physical premises? The author argues that if cloud data is critical infrastructure for diplomatic missions, ensuring its integrity and confidentiality is the digital counterpart of "premises inviolability." This shifts the focus from preventing physical intrusion to protecting against hacking, data leaks, and denial-of-service attacks.

2. **Encryption of Correspondence**: Encrypted digital communications are used in place of traditional mail. While the Vienna Convention guarantees the inviolability of diplomatic communications, modern encryption and quantum communication have raised the technical barriers to communication surveillance. The author emphasizes that legal protection must align with technical protection; that is, legal immunity must not become meaningless due to technical vulnerabilities.

3. **Decentralized Storage of Archives**: When diplomatic documents are stored decentralized in the cloud, which country's law applies? Based on the "principle of functional necessity," the author argues that if a document is essential for performing diplomatic missions, it should be immune regardless of its physical location. This approach prioritizes the functional role of data over its physical location.

4. **Diplomatic Agents and AI**: When AI drafts diplomatic documents or analyzes negotiation data, are these AI actions considered acts of the diplomat? The author maintains the traditional principle that AI should be viewed as a mere tool, with legal responsibility for its outputs attributed to the human diplomat. However, the author points out that if errors or biases stemming from AI's deterministic nature lead to diplomatic disputes, a "clear accountability architecture" is needed to clarify responsibility.

These principles do not apply existing legal provisions as they are, but rather reinterpret them to fit the characteristics of the digital environment under the overarching "principle of functional necessity." The author emphasizes that this reinterpretation is not a unilateral expansion of law, but an essential adaptive process to protect the core functions of diplomatic missions.

## Experiments and Main Findings

This study is not a quantitative research paper presenting statistical analysis or numerical results. Therefore, in this "Experiments and Findings" section, we describe the analytical conclusions derived from the paper's case studies. According to the abstract and provided metadata, the author presents the following findings through four case studies:

1. **Estonia/Luxembourg Data Embassy Case**: Storing diplomatic data on cloud servers rather than physical territory is a pioneering example that redefines the concept of "premises." The author evaluates this case as demonstrating how diplomatic immunity and data protection can function in an environment without physical boundaries, proving the flexibility of existing legal principles.

2. **Barbados Metaverse Embassy Case**: Diplomatic activities in virtual spaces challenge the existing immunity system, which presupposes the physical presence of "diplomatic agents." The author suggests that if interactions within the metaverse produce actual diplomatic effects, they should be included within the scope of legal protection.

3. **Israel Platform Diplomacy Case**: Diplomacy utilizing social media and digital platforms extends the traditional concept of "correspondence." The author points out the issue of potential information manipulation that can occur when platform algorithms filter or amplify diplomatic messages, emphasizing the need for a legal and ethical responsibility structure.

4. **Scandinavian Digital Diplomacy Cases**: Establishing national digital diplomacy strategies is an example of concretizing the "principle of functional necessity" into policy. The author evaluates these advanced cases as potentially contributing to future international standard-setting.

The key finding is that these cases collectively highlight the importance of the "principle of functional necessity." That is, regardless of how technological forms change, the functions required for performing diplomatic missions (data protection, communication security, maintenance of representation) must be guaranteed, and accordingly, the scope of immunity must be functionally redefined. Additionally, it is confirmed that the international community currently relies on voluntary national practices and interpretations rather than formal legal codification, meaning legal uncertainty persists.

## Technical Implications and Limitations

This paper provides important implications for technology developers, policymakers, and international law experts alike.

**Technical Implications**:
1. **Legal Considerations in Security Architecture**: When designing cloud-based diplomatic infrastructure, legal immunity scopes must be considered alongside technical security (encryption, access controls). For example, if data is stored on servers in a third country, a legal response plan must be prepared in advance against access requests from that country's law enforcement agencies.
2. **Clarifying the Limits of AI Responsibility**: When AI is used for diplomatic drafting or negotiation support, technical and legal mechanisms are needed to clarify responsibility in the event of diplomatic disputes caused by AI errors or biases. This requires a technical implementation of an "accountability architecture" that transparently logs AI decision-making processes and maintains audit trails.
3. **Legal Status of Virtual Spaces**: If activities in metaverse or virtual diplomatic spaces produce actual diplomatic effects, new standards are needed to define the legal responsibilities of platform providers and users in those spaces.

**Limitations**:
1. **Lack of Legal Binding Force**: While this paper includes legal analysis and policy recommendations, it does not propose binding international law provisions or regulations. Therefore, its impact depends on academic discussion and voluntary policy adoption by individual nations.
2. **Rate of Technological Change**: Digital technology is evolving extremely rapidly. Technologies mentioned in the paper, such as cloud computing, AI, and the metaverse, could be replaced by new forms within a few years, requiring continuous updates to legal interpretations.
3. **Differences in National Interests**: Interpretations of digital immunity and diplomatic privileges can vary significantly depending on nations' political stances and interests. It remains uncertain whether the "principle of functional necessity" proposed by the author will be accepted by all countries.

**Follow-up Research Questions**:
- What are the specific legal impacts of the physical location of cloud servers on diplomatic immunity?
- Can proposals made by AI in diplomatic negotiations hold legal binding power?
- In the event that diplomatic acts in metaverse spaces lead to actual diplomatic disputes, who is liable for damages?

This paper provides an important conceptual framework for how the diplomatic immunity system should adapt in the digital era. By understanding this legal context, technology developers can preemptively consider legal risks when designing diplomatic-related systems.
