## Why is image analysis in automated STEM experiments challenging?

As automation in Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (STEM) experiments advances, fast image segmentation techniques are required for human interpretation, decision-making, position-selective spectroscopy, and atomic manipulation. Historically, supervised learning with human-labeled data has been the primary approach. This method faces two major bottlenecks. First, it requires large amounts of labeled data and is sensitive to out-of-distribution drift caused by variations in resolution, sampling, and beam shape, leading to sharp performance degradation. Second, the labeling process consumes significant time and human resources, and retraining models to adapt to changes in the experimental environment incurs high costs.

## Reward-based optimization operating in label-free environments

This paper addresses the limitations of supervised learning by proposing and benchmarking a reward-driven optimization workflow for STEM image analysis. This unsupervised method does not require labels, allowing for more robust performance without relying on manual human labeling. Furthermore, it offers fully explainable characteristics; the explainable feedback generated by the model allows humans to review the decision-making process and tune the model by selecting positions on the multi-objective Pareto frontier of the reward function.

## Workflow flow for real-time large-scale STEM data

The proposed method is designed to perform real-time (on-the-fly) image analysis in high-speed, dynamic automated STEM experiments. Based on the conceptual flow presented in the original text, it operates in the following steps:

```flow
Original STEM image input
→ Unsupervised optimization based on reward function (Pareto frontier exploration)
→ Generation of explainable feedback and human review/correction
→ Output of optimized segmentation results and analysis decisions
```

This flow enables the derivation of analysis results without latency in high-throughput experimental environments that process large amounts of data, while also leaving room for human intervention in final decisions.
