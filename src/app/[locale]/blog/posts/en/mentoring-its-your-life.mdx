## What it was

Through KB's youth IT talent programme, I mentored job-seekers' team projects
for three weeks and then served as a judge for the final presentations.

## What I said most often

"Why does that feature need to exist?"

By a wide margin. I expected technical questions;
the actual blockers were almost always one step earlier.

Many teams tried to build twenty features and finished none.
"Keep only what you can finish in three weeks" usually unblocked them.

## What became visible while judging

Hearing twenty-plus presentations back to back, patterns emerged.

**Memorable presentations**
- Explained the problem within thirty seconds
- Had a demo that actually ran
- Were candid about what they didn't finish

**Forgettable presentations**
- Opened with a list of technologies
- Spent a long time on architecture diagrams
- Tried to show every feature and ran out of time

Teams doing technically harder work didn't automatically score better.
**Whether it landed** mattered more.

## What came back to me

Watching from the judging seat, I thought about my own internal presentations.
I also open with the tech stack.

I also thought about why the candid teams earned more trust.
Only from the judge's chair did I feel how much more credible
someone sounds when they know their own limits.

## Takeaway

People say teaching helps you learn. More precisely, it
**forces you to check whether your approach is explainable.**

The points where my explanation stalled were exactly the places
I'd been operating on habit.
