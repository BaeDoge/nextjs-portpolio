## What I picked up

I now work on a channel where customers consult a qualified specialist over video
and subscribe to financial products without visiting a branch.

My part is **identity verification**.
Remote trust product sales and non-face-to-face Star Banking subscriptions
legally require verifying who the customer is.

## The hard part

Two demands sit in direct opposition.

- Customers want it fast and simple
- Regulation and security want it certain

Add one authentication step and drop-off rises.
Remove one and impersonation risk rises.

I initially framed this as "find the balance." In practice it's about deciding
**which segments get strict treatment and which get lenient treatment.**

## Lesson 1 — failure paths vastly outnumber the happy path

There's one success route and dozens of failure routes.

- Glare on the ID card
- Face drifts out of frame
- Poor lighting
- Network drop
- Older ID card format

Treating them all as generic "failure" leaves the customer with no idea what went wrong.
Each needs a distinct reason and a distinct instruction.

That accounts for most of the logic complexity.

## Lesson 2 — some things must never be logged

For debugging I'd love to inspect the failed image. ID images can't be stored.

So instead of images we keep **decisions and metrics**:

- which verification step failed
- similarity score band
- retry count

Tracing causes without the original artefact means deciding *what to record*
much more carefully at design time.

## Lesson 3 — domain knowledge is design

You can't write the code without knowing what the Real Name Financial Transactions Act requires.
Not knowing *why* a step exists leads you to optimise the wrong thing.

This project made it clear that technical skill alone isn't sufficient here.
